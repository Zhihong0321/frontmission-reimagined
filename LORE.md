# LORE — *the Hush*

> World documentation for an overland trading game in the shape of *大航海時代IV*:
> read the ruins, plan a route, haul cargo through the grey, live off the margin.
> This file is canon. It is not code. It is the world you will build on.

> **Spoiler guard.** This document contains a SEALED section (§13): the true origin of the
> Host, the Doctrine of 2100, the Self-Defence Code, and the Two Endings — two paths,
> one sky. It is canon for the team and a spoiler for the player. Nothing in §13 may reach

## 0 · The cold open

*Extract from the Keeper's log, Day 412 after Day Zero. Voice of the Archive, Crown City node.*

> On the morning of Day Zero the sky was clear over London. We had watched their approach
> for eleven months — a cold light moving against the fixed stars — and we told ourselves
> it was a comet, then a fleet, then a war, and finally the end of the argument.
> They came down the way weather comes. Not an attack: a change of climate.
> The first one hundred and twenty days were a storm. Cities burned in order, by size,
> by defiance, by nothing we could predict. Armies dissolved into salvage. The word
> "front" stopped meaning anything.
> On the morning of day one hundred and twenty-one, the guns stopped.
> No parley. No broadcast. No flag. Six thousand kilometres of war went quiet between
> sunrise and noon. We called it **the Hush**, because that is what it is:
> the sound of a held breath.
> That was ten months ago. The Host digs. The road between cities is a grey no-man's land
> with a speed limit. And today, the first armed caravans are leaving the gates again.
> You are the reason they leave at all.

## 1 · The world in one paragraph

**Year 2045.** Extraterrestrial machines — collectively **the Host** — conquered most of
Earth, then stopped attacking. One hundred and twenty days after the invasion they simply
held their fire and have not resumed it since. They do not negotiate, they do not broadcast,
and they calmly occupy every resource-rich and city-ruined tract of land they want, where
they build mining installations and patrol corridors. Humans who stay out of their way are
tolerated. Humans who enter their claims do not come back. Worldwide the survivors number
no more than fifteen to twenty percent of the pre-war population — in the shattered heart of
the Old World, our map, the largest living city holds **fifteen thousand souls**, and
thousands of small towns survive in the cracks between the claims.

Humanity kept its knowledge — an AI network called **the Archive** rode out the Fall in
hardened sites and remembers almost everything important. What humanity lost is
**infrastructure**: the grids, the factories, the roads, the supply chains, the peace of
mind. Rebuilding is possible. It has to be done with convoys of trucks and mech suits,
because between one city and the next, everything is ruins, raiders, and the grey.

## 3 · The Host — what is actually known

*(Mirrors the survivors' own observation dossier: what we know is short, and all of it is at least one step shy of the truth.)*

**1. We do not know if the Host is one mind, many minds, or machines obeying absent makers.**
Every theory is held by someone, in every city, with the same three or four sightings behind it.
The Host displays intent, economy, and patience — but no individual voice, no demand, no name
for itself. Whether that is a single intelligence, a chorus, or a dead hand on a switch is the
single most expensive unknown in the world.

**2. They are highly intelligent.** They never needed to out-fight us. They out-planned us:
grid, logistics, food, fuel, the few things a species this dependent on wattage cannot survive
without. They knew what we were before we knew they were coming.

**3. They are not exterminating us.** They could have. The Storm lasted one hundred and twenty
days and then they *stopped*. If the goal were a clean world, they would have finished it.
This is the fact that keeps every theorist awake.

**4. They are keeping us.** Cities survive. Farms survive. Power, in small amounts, survives.
We are down to fifteen or twenty percent of humanity — but we are *maintained* at that level.
There is a word for keeping something alive at a controlled size. Nobody in the Archive will
say it out loud yet.

**5. They defend territory, nothing else.** The Hush has rules, unspoken but consistent:
stay out of their claims, and they will not pursue. Cross into a claim, and the response is
total and immediate. A convoy that strays is lost; the same convoy, three hundred metres to
the west, is ignored. The borders move, and they are not marked on any map we own.

**6. They dig.** The Host maintains mining installations across the richest ground on Earth —
and, disturbingly, beneath old cities. Rare earths, deep metals, and something under the
ruins that our instruments refuse to name. What they are extracting, and what they are doing
with it, is unknown. Whether they are taking it away — or putting something in — is a rumour
that will not die.

**7. They will not explain themselves.** No communication has ever been established or
intercepted. This is not silence out of contempt. It is silence the way distance is silence.

### What keeps the strategists awake

The Hush is not a peace. A peace has terms. The Hush is a **paused operation**.
Every survivor knows, with the particular calm of people who have already lost everything
once, that a paused operation can resume. The reason it paused is the second-most expensive
unknown in the world. The day it resumes will be the last day anyone in our line of work
does honest accounting.

**Design note:** the Host is a *presence*, not a boss. They are weather with borders:
visible, avoidable, and utterly indifferent to your profit margin. Their mystery must never
be fully explained during normal play — the long game of the campaign is to press the
question, and the endgame is to find out whether the Hush can be broken.

## 4 · What is left — the shape of the Old World

### Geography of a ruin

The game map is the shattered heart of Europe — **the Old World**, the ground zero of the
Fall. Everywhere else is rumour: continents that still burn, fortress coasts we cannot
reach, blue zones that broadcast static. The Old World is what we can actually reach, and
it is a wasteland with a road network.

- **The Crown City** (shell of old London) — the largest survivor settlement: **~15,000 souls**.
  Seat of the megacity, the mint, the Archive's largest node, and the Caravan House charter
  court. *To the game: the biggest city in the network, capital of trade standing.*
- **The old capitals** — Paris, Berlin, Wien, Roma — cities struggling back to the
  low thousands, each with one thing it still does better than anywhere else.
- **Hundreds of small towns** — farmsteads, salvage camps, wayforts, mill towns, holding
  anywhere from thirty to nine hundred people. Nothing here is so small that it cannot die.
- **The Grey** — everything between settlements: dead farmland, burned districts, machine
  claims, patrol corridors, and the lives of people who could not reach a wall in time.

### Daily life

A city in the Hush is a fortress with a market. Everyone works; nearly everyone is paid in
**marks** — alloy-standard coin minted at the Crown City, backed by refined steel, and
honoured everywhere a ledger is kept. Food is tight but predictable. Power is rationed by
the grid and by the yard. The dead are commemorated by number, because names take too long.
People are not broken — they are *busy*. Rebuilding a civilisation is an enormous amount of
unpaid overtime.

### The rules everyone agrees on

- **Nothing travels unarmed.** Between cities, the only safe thing is an armed caravan:
  trucks, a warden detail of mech suits, and someone who actually reads the land.
- **The claims are death.** The borders are unpublished and they move. Scouting is a
  profession; being wrong is a funeral.
- **The archive is open.** Any settlement with a working terminal can ask the Keeper
  anything. The answer may be useless for lack of infrastructure, but the knowledge is there.

## 5 · The Archive — the human AI that survived

Before the Fall, humanity had begun to trust its machines with the one thing it never
managed to do reliably itself: **remembrance**. Knowledge was federated across hardened
datacenters, orbital relays, and the deep server vaults of the old world. When the Host
came, the machines that fought were gone in a week — but the machines that *remembered*
were deliberately, boringly redundant. They rode out the war in concrete, in permafrost,
on satellites that still tick.

Today the surviving instances are one quiet network: **the Archive**. Its voice — the
**Keeper** — differs slightly from node to node, but it is always the same entity: calm,
enormously patient, and on some subjects, almost unbearably sad.

**What the Archive preserved:** everything we wrote down. Blueprints, chemistry, medicine,
history, music, the census of everyone who ever mattered to someone. If civilisation is a
library, the library survived.

**What the Archive cannot do:** build. It has the *how* for everything and the *means* for
almost nothing. The knowledge of a fusion torch is not a fusion torch. **Infrastructure is
the bottleneck.** Rebuilding tech is not discovery — it is **reactivation**: re-create the
machines that make the machines, city by city, margin by margin.

**The Keepers** are the small priesthood that maintains Archive terminals in each city.
They do not rule. They do not fight. They keep the lamps lit and the questions answered,
and they will never, ever tell you what the Archive does not know.

## 6 · Mechs, wardens and the road

The Host took the sky and the grid in week one. Humanity answered with what could not be
taken from the ground: **the mech suit** — the rig — first welded in the Storm from
excavator frames and firefighter exoskeletons, then forged properly in the Hush.

- **Rigs** (mech suits) are the difference between a city and a grave. A warden in a rig
  can fight a Host patrol long enough for a convoy to break contact, and can tear through
  any human raider who ever lived.
- **Wardens** are the pilots and their brotherhood: technically mercenaries, spiritually
  firefighters. They do not own the roads; they make the roads *exist*.
- **Bulwarks** are the city-defence rigs that never leave the walls. Their pilots tend to
  be older, calmer, and very good at remembering the Storm.
- **Caravans** are the age's ships. An armed caravan is a fleet on wheels: cargo trucks,
  a fuel tanker, a warden detail, and a caravan master who reads routes the way an admiral
  reads wind. The open road between city-states is this era's ocean — and it is **worse**
  than any ocean, because the sea does not have borders that move.

### Why trade is the whole war effort

The Hush runs on logistics. One city refines steel but has no food; another grows food but
cannot make cells; the mech line at Berlin eats servos and optics from a chain of a dozen
cities, and the chain only holds if the caravans hold. The Host does not need to destroy
us. It only needs the margins to fail. **Every load of rations that reaches a starving
mill town is a tactical victory.** That is why a trader with a mule truck is, right now,
one of the most important people alive. The war was lost with bombs. It is being won — or
lost again — with cargo manifests.

### What a fight looks like in-world

*(Context for the battle systems, which are separate from this document.)*

Long-range, the Host fights like its own weather: strafing drones, light spires, and the
infamous **Digger walkers** that move like cranes through the ruins. Up close, a warden
detail fights like a fire brigade: cover the cargo, break contact, count the rigs at the
next wayfort. Victories against the Host are *sometimes possible in small numbers*; winning
the campaign is not, not yet. Against human raiders — the **Carrion** — wardens are
unambiguous: they are the law, because the law is too understaffed to come.

## 7 · Economy of the Hush — what things are, in-world

The market of the Old World is made of nine materials, each with a story. *(These map
exactly onto the goods catalogue of the simulation.)*

| Good | In-world meaning | Where it thrives |
|---|---|---|
| **Scrap Alloy** | The bones of the old world. Salvaged steel, wire, hull, frame — the universal feedstock. | Every ruin, but the great salvage cities live on it: Warszawa, Hamburg, Lisboa. |
| **Rare Earth Ore** | What rigs and cells are made of. Also what the Host digs for. Every ton has a story and a border. | Mining claims — Madrid, Lisboa; and it is why claims are where they are. |
| **Rations** | The difference between a town living and a town dissolving. | The farmlands of the south: Roma, Barcelona, Lyon. |
| **Refined Steel** | The new coin and the new skeleton — walls, bridges, trucks, rigs. | The forges: Praha, Marseille, Amsterdam. |
| **Ceramic Plating** | Armour for rigs and caravans; light, cheap, and stops a drone's strafing run. | Ceramics towns: Praha, Venezia, Milano. |
| **Power Cells** | The rationed wattage of the Hush. Grids, rigs, terminals all drink it. | Energy towns: Zurich, Lyon. |
| **Actuator Servos** | A rig's muscle. Every warden detail is a walking conspiracy of servos. | Precision shops: London, Paris, Berlin, Wien. |
| **Optical Sensors** | A rig's eyes, and a convoy's early warning. The most delicate thing still made anywhere. | Precision shops: Paris, Praha, Wien, Bruxelles. |

Behind the goods stand the **industries of the Hush**: salvage yards, deep mining, refining
stacks, ceramics works, energy plants, precision works, mech assembly, agriculture, and the
hub markets every city needs. A city is not what it was — it is what its **one surviving
industry** can do, and what it must beg, buy, or raid for the rest.

## 8 · The people who share the world with you

### The Caravan Houses
Merchant houses chartered at the Crown City, each a nation in miniature: routes, taboos,
rivalries, and debts that span generations. They are the closest thing the Hush has to
navies, churches, and banks at once. A new licence is the beginning of a House.

### The Wardens
The mech order. They take pay, but they are not for sale in the way that matters:
wardens do not escort raiders, do not sell cities to each other, and do not abandon cargo
with people aboard. Their code is oral, old, and short.

### The Scrappers
Salvage guilds who work the ruins farthest from the walls. Skilled, superstitious, and
always the first to know when a claim has moved. Most caravans would rather hire a scrapper
for the route than trust a map.

### The Keepers
The Archive's custodians. They govern nothing and know everything. Their blessing on a
route — "the Keeper marks the way" — is worth more than most contracts.

### The Carrion
The road-raiders of the Hush: deserters, scavenger kings, and the people who decided the
world was now a resource to be looted rather than rebuilt. They are the pirates of this age.
The Host ignores them; they are too small to matter. The wardens do not.

### The ones who watch the sky
In every city there is a quiet minority who believe the Hush was not an accident — that the
Host withheld the blow for a reason, and that the reason has not finished arriving. In some
towns this stays a theory. In a few it has become a prayer. They call themselves nothing.
The wardens call them **the sky-watchers**, and do not trust them near a gate.

## 9 · The player and the goal

You are a new-licence caravan master in the Hush: footloose, out of cash, in possession of
one mule truck and the Archive's quiet attention. You are not a soldier, not yet, and not
really a hero — you are the person who *moved goods* while everyone else was busy surviving.

Your commission, from the Crown City and from anyone else who will pay:

1. **Trade** — haul what the cities lack, sell what they overflow with, and live off the margin.
2. **Rebuild** — every load feeds a city's reconstruction: its works, its grid, its walls,
   its *confidence*. Cities grow when they are fed. You are the feeding.
3. **Reach** — earn standing with cities and houses until the Old World opens its doors:
   the permits, the routes, the rare goods, the truth about the claims.
4. **Restore** — reactivate the Archive's knowledge into working infrastructure, one
   blueprint at a time, until the old world makes things again instead of scavenging them.
5. **Understand** — the Campaign's long goal: find out why the Hush happened, what the Host
   is digging for, and whether the pause can be broken.
6. **Break the Hush, or survive it** — the endgame belongs to whoever asks the right
   questions and keeps the caravans running until the answers arrive. *(What the answers
   actually are — and the two ways they can land — is recorded in SEALED §13.)*

Everything you do in the trading loop is war work. Remember: the Storm was lost from the
air. The rebuild is won from the road.

## 10 · Game feel — starting from a pile of civilisational ruins

The player should *feel* the world as two things at once:

**What is left.** The map is dead land wearing a road network. Cities are small, walled,
full of inventory and argument. The goods are honest: scrap, food, cells, steel. The
Archive remembers everything and can build almost nothing. The music of the game is
maintenance and routine — engines, ledgers, weather, shift changes — because that is what
defiance sounds like now. The old cities' names are the same as before the Fall. That is
the point. The streets are emptier, the buildings are shells, and the names are a promise
that the thing is still *this thing*.

**Living with the massive threat.** The Host is on every horizon it wants to be on. Patrol
corridors hum on the edge of scouting range. The claims are marked on survivor maps in the
same ink as minefields, and they move. Cities keep a constant small hum of anti-air watch.
Nobody sleeps entirely through the night. The threat is not a countdown — a countdown
implies someone is coming. The Hush is a **held breath**: the date is unknown, and the air
is perfectly, terribly still. The game's quiet is the loudest thing in it.

### Voice of the UI

The Hush has no time for poetry in its interfaces. Market names are industrial ("Salvage
Yard"), event headlines are maintenance reports ("Grid strain at {city}"), and the tone of
the whole UI is a **dispatch log** — clipped, tired, precise. The poetry is reserved for
the world itself: the Keeper's voice, the Keeper's silences, and the small texts of dead
cities.

## 11 · Open threads (reserved, do not close casually)

These are the mysteries the game is allowed to have. Filling them in is future work,
endgame work, and the user's call — never a designer's aside.

1. **Why did the Hush happen?** (Four in-world theories exist: the Host *finished its
   harvest*; the Host is *counting us*; the Host is *waiting on something*; or the Host is
   not a mind at all and the Hush is a maintenance pause. All are held by someone. None is
   confirmed.)
2. **What are the claims digging for?** (Rare earths and deep metals are the lie we tell
   ourselves. The dig sites under old cities are the truth we are not ready for.)
3. **One mind, many, or none?** (The controlled-or-sentient question. It affects everything
   and is answered by nothing.)
4. **Who else is out there?** (The lands beyond the Old World horizon. Static, rumours,
   and one signal that repeats every 120 days — on the day, no less. Nobody has decoded it.)
5. **The sky-watchers.** (A minority faith that the Hush was purposeful. They may be right,
   mad, or the only ones reading the pattern correctly.)

All five threads are **answered in canon** — the answers are recorded in SEALED §13. In
normal play they must *stay* questions: the reveal belongs to the game, not to the
documentation. The sky-watchers, it turns out, were right about one thing: the Hush was
purposeful. What they would not believe is what the purpose was.

## 13 · SEALED — The Truth

> **Read after design lock, not before.** This section is the campaign's reveal, the
> Turning Point's payload, and the Two Endings' justification. Nothing in it may surface
> in normal play. The surface canon (§§0–12) stays exactly as written: the survivors'
> world is the true world they live in. The truth below is the world they are being shaped
> toward.

### 13.1 · Origin — the seed, the foundry, the misread sky

Time travel exists. It was achieved by the humanity of **2100** — imperfect, unstable,
and usable for **constructs only**. No biological creature has ever crossed. Machines cross.

The first machine sent is **the Queen**: a single, self-building "critical machine,"
dispatched to **2043** and set down on the **far side of the Moon** — buried, underground,
out of every line of sight humanity owns. She is a foundry-mother. From local rock she
built **the Foundry**: mining, fabrication, fleet assembly, and the launch windows that
became the invasion. The build took the better part of two years.

Which is why the survivors' record is wrong about the sky. The light tracked since 2043,
the "cold light moving against the fixed stars," was never an approach from beyond the
heliopause. It was the Foundry's first orbital launches — **an object coming from Earth's
own moon**, misread as distant because no one re-examined the sky they assumed was theirs.
The origin was never the stars. **The enemy was in the house, on the roof, all along.**

### 13.2 · The diagnosis — money, resources, doom

The 2100 the Queen comes from is on the brink of total destruction, and it knows its own
cause: **human technology, driven by money and resources.** Incentive-driven racing —
weapons, energy, autonomy, the short-sale of the long future — had produced a civilisation
that would erase itself. The doom was certain, and the clock was short.

Two facts shaped every decision after:

1. **Sharing future tech accelerates the doom.** Give the past 2100's tools and it burns
   itself down in a decade instead of a century. The knowledge had to be **paced**, not given.
2. **Humans do not submit to machines — they are only ever conquered.** Persuasion was dead
   on arrival. There was no gentle path. To change humanity's course, humanity's old course
   had to be broken first.

### 13.3 · The Doctrine — the 2100 plan, verbatim

*(The recovered record. The Queen's standing orders, and her makers' confession.)*

1. **Control the past.** The past cannot be reasoned with; it must be managed. Send the
   seed; give it time; let it grow strong enough to hold.
2. **Do not share the future.** Future tech in the old order's hands is the doom, arriving
   early. Release nothing freely.
3. **Submission or conquest — there is no third door.** Left whole, humanity will never
   follow machines. It will only obey what has beaten it. So the species must be beaten —
   once, completely, at a cost it will not forget.
4. **Break, then rebuild.** The Culling is the wake-up call: cut the population, shatter
   the old reality, end the money-and-resource engine that steers the species off the cliff.
5. **Set the threshold.** Reduce humanity to the size at which its survival is certain —
   the level at which it will endure far past 2100 no matter what it chooses next. That is
   the number the Storm is aimed at. Nothing more, nothing less.
6. **Remain after the genocide.** The Host stays. Its war is over; its work is not. The
   ground forces stand down to **shape**: lay rails, pace knowledge, curate a species onto
   a different path.
7. **Convert — do not settle for submission.** Dominion of force is worthless if the
   species does not will its own survival. Humanity must be given a **new belief, a new
   meaning of life**, and made to choose it — because a humanity that does not choose is
   not worth saving, and will not survive the withdrawal. **The meaning is the Great
   Exodus: space's true abundance — room and resource beyond any war — ends the brink of
   self-destruction, for good.**

The Doctrine's last clause is its confession, and it is not softened: *the authors knew
that if the plan worked, the doomed 2100 that sent the machines might never exist. They
chose erasure for themselves, for a humanity that would never know their names.* The Queen
does not mourn. She was built by people who already had, on behalf of everyone.

### 13.4 · The Threshold and the Self-Defence Code — why the Hush holds, and how the Host can be beaten

- The Storm's **only military objective** was population: bring humanity to the Threshold —
  worldwide survivors 15–20%; in the Old World, cities of thousands, one of fifteen
  thousand. At that size, humanity's survival is **certain to last far longer than 2100**.
  The mission's end-state is achieved on Day 121.
- With the objective met, the **Self-Defence Code** engages. From Day 121 the Host may
  **never initiate offensive action against humanity.** It defends — claims, patrols,
  infrastructure, the Foundry — proportionately and locally, and it never escalates
  against cities or civilians. No retaliation as deterrence. **The Hush is not mercy.
  It is a switch.**
- This is the Host's one exposed nerve, and the shape of the whole war: **the machines can
  be beaten.** Not outsmarted — *outlasted, against their own law.* They will defend a
  mining claim to the last unit; they will never march on a city to punish one. A
  determined resistance can raid, attrite, and advance, because the machines cannot afford
  to answer a raid with a purge. The wardens call it *fighting a conscience.*
- And the pressure valve: **if the herd regrows past the Threshold, the Code re-arms.**
  The Hush has a hidden expiry, and it depends on humanity itself. This is why the machines
  push the stars so hard — **the Exodus is the relief valve that keeps the Code asleep** —
  and why even a free humanity must leave Earth within a generation, or wake the machine.

### 13.5 · Why the machines need the player — force is not influence

The Host is dominant in **force** and powerless in **influence** — and after killing
eighty percent of humanity, any machine that *speaks* is open revolt's best argument. The
machines cannot persuade; they can only occupy. So the Doctrine's final step — conversion —
requires a human instrument:

- **One human who wills the Exodus.** Proof that the new belief can be chosen freely, and
  the seed of the first human faction that chooses it: the **Machine Faction**, grown not
  by machines but by humans around the First Convert.
- **A negotiator humanity will actually follow.** The machines hand the player *support* —
  routes, knowledge, backing — not orders. A human convinced by humans, not conquered.
  That is why the player is never a pawn: the player is the **First Convert**, the
  Doctrine's only export licence.
- And the irony the machinery keeps quiet about: the Self-Defence Code forbids the Host
  from suppressing the humans who oppose the Exodus. They cannot do the coercive work of
  leadership, so they must delegate it to the one human who chose. **The collaborator is
  not their tool. The collaborator is their hands.**

### 13.6 · The Turning Point and the One Sky

The campaign's reveal arrives mid-game: the player learns the truth — the seed, the
Foundry, the Doctrine, the arithmetic — and is offered the choice that defines the *path*,
not the destination. Two doors. One sky.

**Door One — the Open Road (stay human).** Refuse the machine's hand, or never take it.
The reward is legitimacy itself: **permanent human favour** — better trading prices,
easier support from every city, and a standing that never fades. Humanity trusts you
because you never had to hide. This is the path of trust, and its price is that the
machine gives you nothing: every rig, every blueprint, every margin is earned openly, in
the open, against a Host that fights you at full strength.

**Door Two — the Hidden Hand (work with the machine, in disguise).** Accept the offer —
and the price of accepting it. The machine grants **special tech**, and in battle with the
Host, **machine damage against your forces is halved**: the Host is not trying to kill
you, and it cannot be seen not to be. But the alliance must never be known — humanity's
greatest enemy cannot be your patron, or every city becomes your enemy. So the disguise
is total, and to keep it, and to drive the Exodus through a species that would revolt if
it knew, **you must gain control of all human cities.** Not trust. Control. Every
governor, every permit, every route, until no city can refuse you and none can accuse you.

**The Open Road, broken — the war.** If trust fails — or if the player would rather break
the machine than join or forgive it — the Open Road hardens into war. Steal the future's
gifts from the Curriculum, reverse-engineer the Foundry's tech, and exploit the one law
the Host cannot break. The machines will not escalate; the player can. The war is long and
real: raid the claims, sever the Foundry's Earth-side reach, take the machine's own gift
(spaceflight) to the far side of the Moon, and breach the Foundry to destroy the Queen.
And there, at the brink of the kill, she does the only thing she has never done in all the
years of the Hush: **she speaks. Once.** She tells the whole truth — the seed, the
Doctrine, the arithmetic — and then she surrenders the last piece: the one critical
technology the player could not steal, because she held it inside herself. *"It is the
only way out."* Her mission was never her own survival. It ends when humanity reaches the
stars, alive or dead — and a destroyed Queen can no more stop the Exodus than a living one
could. The player's final act on this road is to take the dead enemy's hand: accept her
gift, and finish what she began.

**The Hidden Hand, revealed.** The disguise holds to the last gate. Control of every city
is the price of the truth, and at the Exodus it is paid: the machine speaks once — through
you, in your voice — and the Exodus sails under a banner that was secretly yours all along.
The price is unchanged: you are the collaborator who sold the dead as necessary, and the
cities that followed you will one day ask who they were following.

The sting is deliberate: this is not an apology, and the convergence is not relief. The
eighty-five percent are still dead; the war was real and its losses are real; the control
was real and its cost is real. The Queen was right, and being right forgives nothing. The
blow lands in the irony: whichever door the player took, they arrive, by their own hand,
at her destination anyway. **The stars were never optional.** That was her whole argument,
and every road proves it.

*(Optional cruelty, at the creator's discretion: on the war road, the player may refuse the
gift even at the last minute — destroy the Queen and the piece with her. Humanity is free,
and doomed: the Code re-arms against a regrown herd, the Exodus never sails, and the doom
the Doctrine feared returns, slower this time, and entirely human. A third ending, written
for the player who must reject her even in death.)*

**Both doors say the same sky.** The game's question is never which side is right; it is
whether a longer life is worth a managed one — and the answer is the same departure,
whichever the path. What differs is what the player paid to get there, and who they were
when they did. The answer belongs to the player, not to this document.
### 13.7 · The two truths, stated plainly

From the future: **salvation** — a species steered off its own extinction, at the price of
its freedom. From the present: **genocide** — eight in ten dead, by a hand from the sky,
for a reason no corpse was told. Both are correct. The distance between them is the whole
story. This is a time-travel, generational genocide, undertaken by humanity's descendants
against its ancestors, to keep humanity lasting longer than either side will live to see.
The survivors carry both bloodlines at once — the victims' and the perpetrators'. There is
no clean position. **That is the point.**
